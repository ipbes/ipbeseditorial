# Inclusive language

These rules cover gender and disability in English IPBES documents. They add detail to section 3 of `house-style.md`. Where this file and IPBES practice in `house-style.md` differ, `house-style.md` wins.

Sources:

- **Gender:** UN, *Guidelines for gender-inclusive language in English*, https://www.un.org/en/gender-inclusive-language/guidelines.shtml, and its Toolbox page.
- **Disability:** UN Department of Global Communications, *Disability-Inclusive Communications Guidelines* (March 2022). Its terminology table (table 1) is taken from the UN Office at Geneva, *Disability-Inclusive Language Guidelines* (2021), https://www.ungeneva.org/sites/default/files/2021-01/Disability-Inclusive-Language-Guidelines.pdf. For the latest terms, check UNTERM (https://unterm.un.org).

**Decisions and other adopted texts are edited like any other text.** Apply these rules there too, as long as the meaning stays the same. Do not reword direct quotations; flag them instead.

## 1. Gender

The UN guidelines give three strategies. Use them together, and keep the text clear and concise; do not make a sentence clumsy just to avoid a pronoun.

### 1.1 Use non-discriminatory language

- **Forms of address.**
  - Use the title and pronouns that match the person's gender identity. For IPBES officials and experts, check the participant list or the secretariat directory.
  - When a woman's preference is not known, use "Ms.", not "Mrs."
  - Treat people in parallel: if one person is given a surname and title, give the same to the other. Write "Professor Smith and Professor Jones", not "Professor Smith and Madeline".
- **Stereotypes.** Remove expressions that assign roles or traits by gender: "throws like a girl", "women's work", "guests are invited to attend with their wives" (write "with their partners"), "fathers babysit their children" (write "fathers care for their children").
- **Test.** Swap the genders. If the sentence then sounds odd or changes meaning, it is probably biased.

### 1.2 Make gender visible when it is relevant

- Name both women and men ("women and men", "girls and boys") when assumptions might otherwise hide one group: "All of the soldiers, both men and women, responded…"
- **Pairing**, as in "he or she" or "her or his", is acceptable but should be used sparingly. It can distract the reader and make legal or procedural text less precise. If you alternate the order, do it by paragraph or section, not by sentence.
- Write pairs in full. Do not use slash or bracket forms such as "he/she", "his/her" or "(s)he". The UN guidelines mark "his/her" as less inclusive and fix it by omitting the word.
- Keep gender-specific terms where gender is the point, for example "women's rights", "gender equality" and "women and girls".

### 1.3 Do not make gender visible when it is not relevant

**Neutral nouns.**

| Instead of | Use |
|---|---|
| chairman, chairwoman | Chair; in IPBES: Chair, Co-Chair, Vice-Chair (see `house-style.md`) |
| spokesman | spokesperson |
| policeman, policewoman | police officer |
| mankind | humankind, humanity, the human race |
| manpower | staffing, staff, workforce, human resources |
| man-made | human-made, human-caused, anthropogenic, artificial |
| layman | non-specialist, layperson |
| to man (a stand, a post) | to staff, to run |
| fisherman, fishermen | fisher, fishers |

The UN guidelines give the first six rows. The last three follow the same principle and are common in IPBES subject matter; "fishers" is the usual term in IPBES assessments.

**Ways to avoid a generic "he", in rough order of preference for formal text:**

| Technique | Before | After |
|---|---|---|
| Omit the pronoun | "…to continue his/her efforts to strengthen…" | "…to continue efforts to strengthen…" |
| Use "who" | "If a complainant is not satisfied…, he can ask for a rehearing." | "A complainant who is not satisfied… can ask for a rehearing." |
| Use a plural antecedent | "A substitute judge must certify that he has familiarized himself…" | "Substitute judges must certify that they have familiarized themselves…" |
| Use "one" | "…earns less than he would in New York." | "…earns less than one in New York." |
| Rephrase without the clause | "…for 20 years before he may apply…" | "…for 20 years before applying…" |
| Use the passive (sparingly) | "…the situation he is describing." | "…the situation being described." |

- **Singular "they".** The UN guidelines accept "they" or "their" with a singular antecedent only in informal writing such as emails. Do not use it in formal documents. When it refers to a specific person who uses "they", follow that person's preference.
- **Default masculine.** Look for "his" or "he" after "every representative", "each expert", "the Chair", "the author", "a member" and similar words, and rewrite using the techniques above.

## 2. Disability

The UN follows the social model and the human rights-based approach of the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. Disability comes from barriers in society, not from the person. Write about the person, not the impairment.

### 2.1 Person-first language

- Write "persons with disabilities", "a person with a disability" and "children with disabilities". Do not write "disabled persons" or "the disabled".
- "Persons with disabilities" is the standard form in formal UN text. Use "people with disabilities" only in easy-to-read, informal or spoken text.
- Do not modify the term. Avoid euphemisms such as "special needs", "differently abled", "people of all abilities" and "people of determination".
- Mention an impairment only if it is relevant and the person has disclosed it.
- Some organizations of persons with disabilities choose identity-first language ("deaf person"). That is acceptable when quoting or naming them.

### 2.2 Recommended terms and terms to avoid

This table comes from table 1 of the 2022 guidelines.

| Recommended | Avoid |
|---|---|
| person with disability; persons with disabilities; person with [type of impairment] | disabled person, handicapped, person with special needs, handicapable, atypical, person living with a disability, differently abled, people of all abilities, people of determination, those with disabilities |
| person without impairment; broader population | normal, healthy, able-bodied, typical, whole, of sound body/mind |
| with a disability, impairment or condition | suffers from, afflicted by, stricken by, troubled with, affected by |
| person with an intellectual disability or impairment | retarded, simple, slow, brain-damaged, intellectually challenged, subnormal, of unsound mind, feeble-minded, mentally handicapped |
| person with a psychosocial disability | insane, crazy, maniac, psycho, lunatic, demented, mentally deranged, mentally ill, person with mental health problems |
| deaf person; person who is deaf; person with a hearing disability, impairment or loss; hard-of-hearing person | the deaf, hearing impaired, deaf and dumb, deaf and mute |
| blind person; person who is blind; person with a visual disability or impairment; person with low vision; deafblind person | the blind, partially sighted |
| person with a physical disability or impairment | crippled, invalid, deformed, lame, physically challenged, person with physical limitations, home-bound, bedridden |
| wheelchair user; person who uses a wheelchair; person with a mobility disability or impairment | confined to a wheelchair, restricted to a wheelchair, wheelchair-bound |
| person of short stature; little person | midget, dwarf, stunted |
| person with Down syndrome | mongoloid, special person, Down |
| person with albinism | albino |
| person affected by leprosy | leper, leprosy patient |
| person who uses a communication device or an alternative method of communication | non-verbal, cannot talk |
| accessible parking, accessible toilet | disabled parking, handicapped parking, handicapped bathroom |

### 2.3 Ableist expressions and stereotypes

- **Metaphors.** Do not use disability terms as metaphors or adjectives: "blind to the evidence", "turn a deaf ear", "lame excuse", "crippling debt", "paralysed by indecision", "schizophrenic policy", "OCD about details". In IPBES text, "blind spot" (a gap in knowledge) can usually become "gap" or "overlooked area".
- **Portrayal.** Do not present persons with disabilities as vulnerable, a burden, less valuable, dangerous, or heroic simply for living with a disability.
- **Groups.** Do not list persons with disabilities only among "vulnerable groups" without explanation. Where the text is about barriers, name the barriers. This is a suggestion to the author, not a silent edit.

### 2.4 Accessible documents

These checks come from the 2022 guidelines and WCAG 2.1. They apply to Word documents the secretariat produces.

- **Headings and styles.** Use Word's built-in heading and paragraph styles, not manual bold or font size, so that screen readers can navigate the document.
- **Alt text.** Every image, figure, map and logo needs alt text of one or two sentences that gives the image's purpose. Do not start with "image of" or "graphic of". Decorative elements, such as motifs and rules, are marked as decorative.
- **Colour.** Do not rely on colour alone to convey meaning in figures or tables. Text contrast should be at least 4.5:1, or 3:1 for large text (18 pt regular or 14 pt bold and above).
- **Tables.** Use a header row, repeated across pages. Avoid merged or split cells where possible, and do not put tables inside tables.
- **Links.** Use descriptive link text rather than bare long URLs. In masthead documents, which follow UN practice and show URLs in full, keep the URL.
- **Accessibility Checker.** Run Review > Check Accessibility in Word before finalizing.
- **Fonts and alignment.** The guidelines recommend sans serif fonts and left-aligned text. For official documents, the IPBES Word template's fonts and alignment take precedence (see `report-structure.md`). Apply this recommendation to web content, presentations and informal material.

## 3. Quick checklist

1. Is there a generic "he" or "his", or a "he/she" or "his/her"?
2. Are there "-man" words (chairman, mankind, manpower, man-made, fishermen)?
3. Are women and men named and titled in parallel, with "Ms." used where the preference is unknown?
4. Is the language person-first ("persons with disabilities"), with no euphemisms?
5. Are there terms from the "avoid" column in table 2.2, or ableist metaphors?
6. Do the images have alt text, and do the figures work without colour?
7. Does any of this fall in a direct quotation? If so, flag it rather than changing it.
